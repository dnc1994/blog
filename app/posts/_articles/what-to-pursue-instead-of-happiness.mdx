I recently read David Wootton's essay "[The Impossible Dream](https://www.laphamsquarterly.org/happiness/impossible-dream)" in Lapham's Quarterly, and one point in it has stuck with me. The Declaration of Independence lists "the pursuit of Happiness" as a right. Wootton finds that odd, because people chase happiness the way water runs downhill, and nobody needs a right to do something they can't help doing. His reading is that the right only makes sense as a right to the preconditions: liberty, speech, opportunity, room to decide for yourself what a good life is. A government can't give you happiness. It can protect the conditions under which you might find some.

That made me wonder what the actual theories of happiness say, and whether any of them are actionable. After going through the main ones, I think Wootton's reading of the Declaration also works as personal advice. Happiness is a bad thing to aim at directly. The conditions that produce it are a much better target, and the rest of this post is my attempt to explain why.

## "Happiness" means several things

Philosophers don't agree on what happiness is, but they do agree on how the disagreement divides. The [Stanford Encyclopedia entry on well-being](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/well-being/) sorts the theories into three families. Hedonism says a good life is one that feels good. Desire-satisfaction theories say it is one where you get what you want. Objective-list theories say some things are good for you whether or not you want them: friendship, knowledge, achievement, health.

I expected this to be hair-splitting, but the three come apart all the time. A vacation feels good and changes nothing about where a life is going. Writing a book is something a person can badly want while most of the days spent writing it are unpleasant. And someone who is lonely may sincerely not want to see anyone, even though company is what they are missing.

Psychologists ended up with a similar split. Ed Diener's [1984 review](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.95.3.542), which set up subjective well-being as a field of study, separates how people feel day to day from how they rate their life as a whole. The two are measured separately and don't always move together. The World Happiness Report [ranks countries by life evaluation](https://worldhappiness.report/faq/) and not by mood, because evaluations are steadier and track people's circumstances more closely. A hard stretch of work that you believe in is the usual example: the days are bad and you would still say life is going well.

So "how can I be happier?" is several questions. It could mean I feel bad lately, or my life is headed somewhere I don't like, or I want things that won't do me any good. Advice aimed at one of these does little for the others, which I suspect is why so much happiness advice seems not to work.

## Aiming at it directly doesn't work either

Say you settle on one meaning and go after it. Several separate lines of research say this goes badly.

One problem is prediction. Daniel Gilbert and Timothy Wilson coined "miswanting" for the fact that people are poor at forecasting how they will feel about the things they want. My reading of what follows from that is that we chase stand-ins. A promotion is a stand-in for having some control over your work, and an audience is a stand-in for respecting yourself. The stand-in is easier to go after than the real thing, so it is what gets pursued. The strange case is when you get it and feel let down anyway, and nothing in the plan went wrong.

Then there is adaptation. The study everyone cites is [Brickman, Coates, and Janoff-Bulman (1978)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.36.8.917), which interviewed 22 lottery winners, 29 people paralyzed in accidents, and a control group. On a 0-to-5 scale the winners rated their happiness at 4.00 against 3.82 for the controls, a difference too small to mean anything, and they reported less pleasure from ordinary things like breakfast or talking with a friend. The study is often summarized as "the accident victims bounced back too." They didn't. They came in at 2.96, well below the controls, though higher than most people would guess.

It is a small study and I wouldn't lean on it much. A later and much larger study of Swedish lottery winners, "Long-Run Effects of Lottery Wealth on Psychological Well-Being," found that winners did stay more satisfied with their lives. What I'd keep is the more modest claim: we overestimate how much, and for how long, a windfall will change how we feel.

The most direct evidence is an experiment. In a [2011 paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022010), Iris Mauss and colleagues got one group of participants to place a high value on happiness and then showed everyone a cheerful film clip. That group ended up less happy than the control group, and the reason was disappointment. They had a standard for how good they should feel, and the clip didn't meet it.

The oldest version of the point is about attention. Csikszentmihalyi's flow is the state of being absorbed in something hard enough to need all of your attention, and people often describe it as the best time they have. It only lasts while you aren't thinking about yourself, so checking whether you are enjoying it tends to end it. John Stuart Mill said the same thing in his autobiography in 1873: "Ask yourself whether you are happy, and you cease to be so."

None of these is decisive alone, and the adaptation result is weaker than its reputation. Together they were enough to convince me that trying harder at the direct approach is the wrong fix.

## Work on the generators

What I took from all this is that happiness is not one quantity to push up; it is a set of signals, each produced by some underlying good. I'll call those goods **generators**. Most of the theories above read better to me as claims about which generators exist than as competing definitions.

The body is the obvious one, and it is what hedonism gets right: sleep, exercise, daylight, food, not being in pain or under constant stress. A lot of unhappiness that feels like a crisis of meaning is tiredness.

Self-determination theory names three more. [Ryan and Deci](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.55.1.68) argue that people do well to the extent they have autonomy, competence, and relatedness, which in plainer terms means some say over their own life, the sense of getting better at something, and people who know them. Of everything I read this was the easiest to apply, because each of the three suggests its own fix.

Aristotle's tradition adds commitment. Meaning and self-respect seem to come from being devoted to something you endorse, and I don't know of a way to get them by working on your mood.

The Stoics, Epicureans, and Buddhists start from different places and arrive at similar advice, which is to want fewer things that are outside your control. If I can only be at peace when everything goes my way, I won't be at peace often, and getting one more thing I want doesn't change that.

These are all conditions I can work on, which is why Wootton's political point carries over to a single life. They also turn "how can I be happier?" into questions that have answers. Is this about my body, or about where my life is going? Am I after a stand-in for something I haven't named? Which is short: autonomy, competence, or people? Am I comfortable and committed to nothing? The answers point to different actions.

I should add two caveats. Calling happiness a side effect doesn't mean it is luck. Working on generators makes it more likely; it just does so by aiming at something more specific. And none of this means "fix your attitude." [Diener, Lucas, and Scollon's 2006 review](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.61.4.305) of the adaptation research concludes that people do not adapt to everything, and that unemployment and serious disability can lower well-being for years. Some circumstances have to be changed, and I'd distrust any theory of happiness that says otherwise.

## Happiness as a signal

So what is the feeling for, if not to be chased? I think of it as a signal. When it shows up, some generator is working. When it fades, one of them is failing, and often I notice the fading before I can say which one. That makes it worth paying attention to. It doesn't make it a good target, for the reason engineers call Goodhart's law: a measure that becomes a target stops measuring what it used to.

Wootton reads the Declaration as promising the preconditions and leaving the pursuit to each person. For my own life I would go one step further and drop the pursuit as well. I'd rather look after the conditions, and take happiness as a signal that they are in decent shape.
