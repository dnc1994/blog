I started thinking about this from a narrow problem: how to improve the answers a language model gives.

When someone says an AI answer is "bad," the word does not help much. Bad how? It might have invented a fact, missed what the user wanted, answered at the wrong level of abstraction, refused something harmless, complied with something it should have refused, lost the thread from three turns ago, or solved the benchmark-shaped version of the task instead of the real one.

Those are different failures. They usually need different fixes. Until you split "bad" into kinds, you can collect thumbs-downs and average scores forever without learning very much. You are measuring something you have not yet understood.

That was the local problem. But the more I thought about it, the less AI-specific it felt. A domain becomes workable when you learn which distinctions matter.

A taxonomy is not a filing system. It is a theory of what differences matter.

Two things follow from this. First, taxonomies do different jobs, and a lot of arguments about them are really arguments about which job we expect them to do. Second, categories do not only describe the world. They act on it.

## Taxonomies do different jobs

The cleanest job a taxonomy can do is prediction.

The periodic table is the obvious example. It is not just a list of elements. It arranges them so that recurring properties line up. Mendeleev's version was strong enough that he could leave gaps and describe the properties of elements that had not been discovered yet. That is the rare case where a classification system organizes known facts so well that it points toward unknown ones.

Most taxonomies do not work like that. They do more ordinary work, which is easy to undervalue if we keep comparing them to the periodic table.

Some taxonomies coordinate. The International Classification of Diseases gives hospitals, governments, researchers, and insurers a shared way to record illness and death. A diagnosis code is crude compared with a person's actual condition, but the crudeness is partly the point. The system needs many institutions to record similar cases in similar ways. Otherwise the data cannot be compared.

Some taxonomies allocate. Triage categories rank patients when there are more people needing help than there are people or supplies to help them. The point is not to understand each patient in full. The point is to decide what to do first under scarcity.

Some taxonomies govern. A platform's categories for spam, harassment, hate speech, self-harm, scams, and other harms shape more than a moderation report. They affect which posts get queued for review, what moderators are trained to notice, which appeals are possible, and what the platform can later claim about its own behavior. If a harm has no category, it is harder to count. If it is filed under the wrong one, it is harder to understand.

This distinction between jobs explains why some debates go in circles.

Take the DSM, the manual of mental disorders. A common criticism is that its diagnoses do not cut reality at clean joints. Conditions overlap. Boundaries move between editions. Symptoms vary person by person. That criticism is fair if we are judging the DSM as a predictive scientific taxonomy, something closer to the periodic table.

But the DSM also has other jobs. It gives clinicians and researchers a shared language. It gives insurers and hospitals a way to decide what counts as a diagnosable and reimbursable condition. It shapes what research gets funded and what kinds of suffering become legible to institutions. A category can be scientifically messy and still have administrative force.

That does not make the DSM good or bad by itself. It means the first question should be: good for what?

## Categories act

The examples above are not passive descriptions. Triage categories change who gets treated first. Diagnostic categories can unlock insurance or flatten a person's experience into criteria. Disease codes affect what a health system can measure. Platform harm categories shape enforcement.

This is the part of classification that writers like James Scott, Bowker and Star, and Ian Hacking have explored from different directions. Categories are not neutral labels placed on top of reality. They become part of the reality people have to live inside.

A category can be a door. It can also be a wall.

This is easy to miss because classification often looks like paperwork. But the act of naming the kinds is already a decision about what a system can see and what it can do.

## "Good" depends on the job

People often ask whether a taxonomy is true. Sometimes that is the right question. If the taxonomy is supposed to reveal hidden structure or support prediction, truth is the standard.

But many taxonomies are built for other purposes. A taxonomy used for reimbursement, policy, moderation, shelf placement, or emergency response should be judged by how well it serves that purpose and what damage it creates while doing so.

A classification system built to coordinate millions of cases will flatten individual cases. That is not always a defect. The ICD has to treat a particular illness as an instance of a type, or it cannot make health data comparable across hospitals and countries. The same flattening can be useful for an insurer and harmful in a therapist's office. The operation is similar. The context changes the verdict.

This is why the wrong taxonomy can be worse than no taxonomy. With no categories, you are in fog, and at least you know you are in fog. With bad categories, you get the comfort of precision before the distinctions have been earned.

A team invents five labels for model failures and starts charting them. The chart looks like progress, even if the labels are just renamed vibes. A product team decides users churn for five reasons, and from then on every messy reason has to fit one of the five. The map stops reporting the territory and starts overruling it.

## Expertise means using the map without worshipping it

Expertise is partly the ability to live between two dangers: not enough categories and too much faith in the categories you have.

Beginners often lack distinctions. They see only the gross category: the code is broken, the essay is bad, the model failed, the patient is sick.

Intermediates often have the opposite problem. They learn the official taxonomy and start treating every case as an instance of it. The boxes feel more real than the examples.

Experts can use the taxonomy while also seeing its seams. They know which distinctions are load-bearing, which are teaching devices, which are political compromises, and which are probably temporary. They do not reject the map. They just remember what it was made for.

This is also a useful way to enter a new field. The beginner asks, "What should I read?" A better question is, "What distinctions do insiders make that outsiders miss?"

Often the answer is not in the textbook. It shows up in workflow. What does a practitioner check first? Which cases do they refuse to lump together? Which failure modes have names? Which categories exist because they track reality, and which exist because an institution needs to process the world at scale?

## Every taxonomy is temporary

Every taxonomy is useful only under some set of purposes and assumptions.

Linnaean ranks let naturalists coordinate observations across the world for a long time. Later, evolutionary thinking showed that some of those categories were useful handles rather than faithful pictures of ancestry. That is a common lifecycle. A taxonomy helps people see for a while. Then, if the field keeps moving, the same taxonomy may start getting in the way.

The best taxonomy is not the one that pretends to be reality. It is the one that is useful enough to act with, explicit about the job it is doing, and easy enough to revise when its distinctions stop working.

That is why taxonomy work can look boring from the outside and feel important from the inside. Naming kinds looks like administration. But when the names are right, they turn vague complaints into cases, cases into patterns, and patterns into decisions.

A taxonomy is a theory of what differences matter. To improve one is to improve what you are capable of noticing.
