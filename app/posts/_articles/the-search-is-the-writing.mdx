Everything below came out of that dump and a few rounds of back-and-forth. My view is that the essence of my writing now lives in the **pre-writing**: the collecting and connecting that happened before I typed anything, of which the dump is the residue. By the time I opened the chat, pre-writing had already cut the space of posts I could write down to a small region, and the model only had to search what was left. That is also why someone else with the same model would not get this post. Their pre-writing is different, so they would be searching a different region.

Working this way can look like cheating, and the rest of this post is why I don't think it is.

## Two descriptions of one thing

The account of creativity I hear most often is that it means connecting fields that look unrelated. Teng Bao's [Creativity Is a Search Problem](https://www.tengbao.me/blog/creativity-is-a-search-problem/) gives a different one: every possible work already sits somewhere in an enormous space of possible works, and creating is "using heuristics to iteratively reduce a large search space" until what remains is good enough. Spelling and grammar are strict heuristics, plot and mood are loose ones, and each choice you make throws away most of what was left.

These usually get presented as two separate ideas, but they describe the same activity. Most heuristics tell you to look near where you already are: the next sentence should follow from this one, the next post should resemble the last. A connection between distant fields is a heuristic that tells you to look somewhere far away, in a region nobody working on your topic has a reason to visit. It is still search. It just takes a longer step.

## Generating was always the cheap half

The [monkeys at their typewriters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_monkey_theorem) will eventually produce Hamlet, and nobody has ever thought that makes the monkeys playwrights. Borges's [Library of Babel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Library_of_Babel) holds every possible book, and its librarians despair because holding all of them is the same as holding none.

There is also a version that actually happened. In 2020 Damien Riehl and Noah Rubin wrote a program that enumerated every 8-note, 12-beat melody, about 68 billion of them, saved them to a hard drive, and [released them into the public domain](http://allthemusic.info/). Their point was about copyright law. The side effect is a physical object that contains every pop hook of that shape, including the ones nobody has written yet, and it has not put a single songwriter out of work. Having all the melodies was never the hard part; knowing which one to keep is the job.

Language models did to prose what that hard drive did to melodies. I can ask for twenty competent openings and have them in a minute. Producing candidates stopped being scarce, and recognition did not get any cheaper. Teng Bao's post is mostly about how heuristics shrink the space. The question I care about more is where one particular person's heuristics come from, because that is the part the model does not supply.

## Pre-writing is where the space shrinks

swyx's [Mise en Place Writing](https://swyx.io/writing-mise-en-place) borrows the kitchen practice of preparing every ingredient before you cook. His argument is that writing should be split the same way: pre-writing is a constant, low-intensity habit of capturing ideas and attaching links and quotes to them as they turn up, and drafting is a separate sitting that goes fast because everything is already on the counter.

In his version the drafting is still a real block of hours. In mine it has mostly disappeared. I keep notes, the notes collect links, some of the notes turn out to be about the same thing, and at some point I pour all of it into a model and we refine it together.

What I had wrong was what pre-writing produces. I thought of my notes as ingredients, raw material that would end up in the dish. Look at the dump at the top of this post: almost none of its prose survived. What it contains is a list of decisions. It says which two framings I believe are secretly one, which examples count as evidence, which other post this one reinforces, and that my own workflow is the case study. Each of those rules out an enormous number of possible posts, and together they leave very few. The prep, in my kitchen, is less about chopping onions than about deciding what is for dinner.

That is the sense in which the writing is in the pre-writing. By the time the dump existed, the search space was small enough that a full draft of this post existed within a quarter of an hour of my sending it. The quarter of an hour was not where the work was.

It has a section on novelty, and that section already says that creativity is a search problem and that the draft is the search result. ## Ripe

The phrase in the dump I can explain least is "when the time is ripe."

The best description I have is in the same terms as everything above. Early on, a new note could belong to any of a dozen posts, so every note opens options. Later, the notes start closing them. A set of notes is ripe when the next thing I save only fits one post, and I can say in a sentence what the notes have in common. For this one the sentence was that Teng Bao and swyx were describing the two halves of what I do every time I write.

That is a description after the fact. I am not sure it would help me predict the next one, and I do not really have a system for it.

## What the search frame leaves out

Search assumes the space and the target hold still, and the best work does not respect that. [Margaret Boden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Boden) separates exploring a conceptual space from transforming it, and the works people remember longest tend to be the ones that changed what counted as good. Teng Bao makes a related concession with *Guernica*: a model can find its way to what the painting looks like without any grasp of why it mattered.

I think this objection is correct and I do not have an answer to it. A blog post that connects two other blog posts is exploration, and I am comfortable with that. I would be more careful making the same claim about a novel.

## Whose search

Claude produced most of the sentences in this post, so "did AI write this?" has an easy answer and it is not a very informative one. A more useful test is to hold the model fixed and change the pre-writing. Someone who asks the same model for an essay on creativity and search will get a competent essay, and it will not be this one, because a hard drive of melodies and a post about kitchen prep were never sitting next to each other in their notes. Run it the other way and hand a stranger my dump, and I expect they would land close to this post without knowing anything else about me. Both results point at the same place for where the writing is.

The dump at the top is 145 words long and badly punctuated. I think it is the part of this post that is most mine.
