I started thinking about this from a narrow place: trying to improve the answers a language model gives.

When someone says an AI answer is "bad," the word is almost useless. Bad how? It might have invented a fact, missed what the user actually wanted, answered the literal question at the wrong level of abstraction, refused something harmless, complied with something it shouldn't have, lost the thread of what was said three turns ago, or produced the benchmark-shaped version of a task instead of the real one. Each is a different failure with a different fix. Until you split "bad" into kinds, you can collect thumbs-downs and average scores forever without improving anything, because the thing you're measuring hasn't been carved into parts yet.

That's the local problem. The longer I sat with it, the less AI-specific it felt. It's a general feature of understanding: a domain becomes workable when you learn which distinctions matter. And the structure that holds those distinctions has a boring name and a surprising amount of hidden power.

A taxonomy is not a filing system. It is a theory of what differences matter.

I want to make two claims about taxonomies that I don't think are obvious, and that changed how I read the ones I deal with every day. The first is that taxonomies do fundamentally different *jobs*, and most arguments about a particular taxonomy are secretly arguments about which job it owes you. The second is that categories don't just describe the world — they act on it.

## Taxonomies do different jobs

Start with the most flattering job a taxonomy can do: **predict**.

The periodic table is the high example. It isn't a tidy list of elements sorted for classroom posters; it arranges them so that recurring properties line up, and the arrangement was confident enough that Mendeleev could leave gaps and describe the properties of elements no one had found yet. The table became a wager: if these categories are right, reality should have missing pieces in exactly these places. It did. That is the rare ideal — a taxonomy that compresses what's known so well it tells you what to look for next.

Most taxonomies never predict an element. They earn their keep doing humbler jobs, and it's worth naming them, because they're easy to mistake for failed versions of the periodic table when they're actually doing something else entirely.

Some **coordinate**. The International Classification of Diseases is a vast bureaucratic map of illness and death, and it is not trying to be beautiful. What it does is let a hospital in one country, a statistics office in another, and an insurer in a third all record the same event the same way. Without shared categories, every institution sees only its own local blur. With them, health becomes countable across borders and decades. The job is not insight; it's agreement at scale.

Some **allocate**. Triage categories don't describe patients so much as rank them for scarce attention: who is treated now, who can wait, who is beyond saving with the resources on hand. In ordinary life we like to treat each person as a full individual case. A mass-casualty event makes that impossible, and the taxonomy steps in precisely to convert overwhelming complexity into an order of action. It doesn't picture the patients. It divides the doctor's time.

Some **govern**. A platform's taxonomy of harm — spam, harassment, hate speech, self-harm, scams — does far more than sort posts after the fact. It shapes the enforcement queue, the moderator's training, the appeals process, the automated classifier, the transparency report, and the public argument about whether the platform is fair. A harm with no category is hard to count. A harm filed under the wrong one is hard to understand. The categories don't follow the policy; they *are* the policy.

Once you separate these jobs, a surprising number of stubborn arguments come apart cleanly.

Take the long fight over the DSM, the manual of mental disorders. Critics point out that its categories don't cut reality at clean joints — that diagnoses blur, overlap, and shift between editions — and conclude it's a bad taxonomy. But that critique judges the DSM by the *predict* standard, the periodic-table standard: are these carvings real, will they reveal hidden structure? That was never the DSM's main job. Its jobs are to coordinate (give clinicians and researchers a shared language), to allocate (decide what counts as a billable, treatable, reimbursable condition), and to govern (structure what research gets funded and what suffering gets recognized). Most of the heat in that debate is a job mismatch: one side asking *is it true?* while the thing is built to answer *can we all act on it together?* Both questions are fair. They are not the same question, and a taxonomy can ace one while failing the other.

## Categories act

Notice what every one of those examples has in common. In none of them is the taxonomy merely sitting there describing. Triage divides attention. The harm taxonomy routes enforcement. The diagnosis unlocks insurance and validates — or flattens — a life. The disease code decides what a health system can even see itself doing.

This is the second claim, and unlike the first I can't take credit for it: it's the thing James Scott, and Bowker and Star, and Ian Hacking each circled from different directions — the recognition that classification is an exercise of power, that to categorize people and problems is to *make up* the world they then have to live in. Whoever defines the categories tends to define the visible problem. A hiring rubric, a disease code, an academic discipline, a product dashboard — each turns a messy reality into administrable units, and everything that falls outside the grid quietly disappears.

So a category can be a door. It can also be a wall. That's the part that's easy to miss when a taxonomy looks like neutral bookkeeping: the act of naming the kinds is already a decision about what the system is allowed to do.

## "Good" is a question about the job, not about truth

Put the two claims together and a question that usually traps people comes loose. The question is: which taxonomies are good? People instinctively reach for *is it true?* — does it carve nature at its joints? But that's only the right test for a taxonomy whose job is to predict. For the others, the standard is functional: a taxonomy is good *relative to the job you need it to do.*

A classification built to coordinate millions of cases at scale will flatten the individual. That isn't a flaw to apologize for; it's the price of the job. The ICD has to treat your particular illness as an instance of a type, or it can't make health countable across a continent. The same flattening that's correct for an insurer is genuinely harmful in a therapist's office, where the job is to see one person clearly rather than to compare them to everyone else. Same logical operation, opposite verdict, because the job changed.

This is why "the wrong taxonomy" is worse than no taxonomy at all. The absence of categories leaves you in fog, and at least fog announces itself. The wrong categories give you a confident, well-labeled map of the wrong city — the comfort of precision before the underlying distinctions have been earned. A team invents five names for model failures and starts charting them, and the chart feels like progress even when the labels are just vibes wearing lab coats. A product team decides users churn for five reasons, and from then on every messy real reason gets jammed into one of the five. The map stops reporting the territory and starts overruling it.

## Living between the two dangers

Maybe expertise is partly the skill of living between those dangers — the fog and the false map — and the shape of that skill is not what you'd guess.

You'd expect expertise to be a straight line: the more you know, the more distinctions you have. It's closer to a U. Beginners lack distinctions and see only the gross category — the code is broken, the essay is bad, the model failed, the patient is sick. Intermediates have the opposite problem: they've learned the official taxonomy and mistake it for reality, sorting every case into a named box with a confidence the boxes don't deserve. Experts come out the far side and hold both at once. They use the categories fluently *and* see the seams — they know which distinctions are load-bearing, which are pedagogical conveniences, which are political compromises, and which are quietly waiting to be replaced. The intermediate trusts the map most. The expert trusts it precisely as far as its job allows.

That's also the most useful question to ask when entering a new field. The beginner asks, "What should I read?" The better question is, "What distinctions do insiders draw that outsiders collapse?" Often the answer isn't in the textbook; it's in the workflow. What does a practitioner check first? Which cases do they refuse to lump together? Which failure modes have names? And — the revealing one — which categories exist because they're true, and which exist because an institution needs to process the world at scale?

## Every taxonomy is a loan

There's a last thing the U-curve points at, and it's the note I want to end on rather than a checklist of virtues.

Every taxonomy is indispensable without being final. Linnaean ranks let naturalists coordinate observations across the whole world for two centuries — and then evolutionary thinking revealed that some of those tidy categories were useful handles rather than faithful pictures of ancestry. This is the recurring life-cycle: a taxonomy helps people see for a while, and then becomes one of the things that stops them from seeing. The categories that let you notice are, eventually, the categories you have to break to notice more.

So the best taxonomy is not the one that pretends to be reality. It's the one that's useful enough to act with, honest enough to name the job it's doing, and humble enough to show where it fails — because it remembers that every classification is a compression, and every compression is a loan taken out against a future revision.

This is why taxonomy work looks like administration from the outside and feels like power from the inside. Naming kinds looks like paperwork. But when the names are right, they turn vibes into cases, cases into patterns, and patterns into decisions. A taxonomy is a theory of what differences matter. To improve one is to improve what you are capable of noticing.
