I used to think a playlist was something you made one song at a time.

There was pleasure in that. Every inclusion felt like a small declaration of taste; every exclusion felt like discipline. A good playlist was not just a container for songs. It was evidence that I had listened carefully, chosen deliberately, and arranged some part of the world into the shape I wanted.

But it was also tiring.

At some point YouTube Music recommendations became good enough that I stopped feeling the need to control every song myself. Not perfect. Sometimes it would drift too mainstream, repeat itself, or miss the exact mood I had in mind. But most of the time it understood enough. It could carry the session forward without me constantly supervising it.

The surprising emotion was not disappointment. It was relief.

I did not feel like I had lost agency. I felt like I had been released from a particular kind of precision burden: the belief that, for the experience to count as mine, every micro-decision had to pass through me.

Generative AI is starting to create the same shift in much larger domains.

For a long time, I hesitated to translate my writing. I wanted people who do not read English to be able to read my posts, but translation felt too costly. A careful human translation would take time and judgment. A rough machine translation felt like it would flatten the voice, miss the nuance, and represent the thought poorly. So the default was: nothing gets translated.

Now the tradeoff looks different. An AI-translated version may still lose something. It may smooth out a sentence I meant to leave jagged, choose a word that is close but not quite right, or miss a cultural resonance. But if 95% of the thought reaches someone who otherwise would get 0%, the loss starts to feel acceptable. Sometimes it feels obviously worth it.

This is the useful frame: many AI systems are lossy compression layers over intention.

A music recommender compresses your taste into a stream of songs. Machine translation compresses your voice across languages. A summarizer compresses a text into the parts it predicts you need. An assistant compresses a workflow into prompts and defaults.

Lossy compression is not the same as corruption. A JPEG throws away information too, but that is what lets the image travel. The question is not whether something was lost. Something always is. The question is whether the lost detail mattered more than the new reach, speed, or freedom that compression made possible.

This is the part that feels liberating: not every preference deserves exact execution. Some precision is care. Some precision is craft. Some precision is safety. But some precision is just vanity, anxiety, or an old self-image of control.

The hard part is telling these apart.

I still do not want to delegate understanding. I do not want an AI to decide what I believe, or to compress away the argument-critical part of a book, or to make a high-stakes factual claim on my behalf without inspection. In those cases, the lost detail may be the whole point.

But for many surfaces of life, the alternative to lossy compression is not pristine human judgment. It is delay, fatigue, narrowing, or nothing at all. No translated post. No organized notes. No playlist playing while I cook because I am still deciding what belongs in it.

Accepting lossy compression is liberating because it changes the standard from "does this match my internal ideal exactly?" to "does this preserve what matters enough to be useful?"

That is a lower standard, but not a worse one. It is often the standard that lets things exist.

Preserve precision where the loss would change the meaning. Accept compression where precision is mostly a way of keeping yourself trapped in the loop.
